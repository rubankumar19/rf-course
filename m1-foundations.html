// MODULE 01 — FOUNDATIONS

Signals, dB,
and the RF World

Before you touch an instrument, you need to think like an RF engineer. That means understanding why we measure power in logarithms, what a spectrum really shows, and what the noise floor actually means. Everything else in this course builds on these ideas.

LESSONS 5 TOPICS Signals · dBm · Spectrum · Noise · Impedance

What is a signal, really?

At its most fundamental level, an RF signal is a voltage or current that oscillates in time at a particular rate — the frequency, measured in Hertz. A Wi-Fi signal at 2.4 GHz is oscillating 2,400,000,000 times every second. The simplest possible signal is a pure sine wave — a single frequency, constant forever. The moment you modulate it (put data onto it), you spread energy across a range of frequencies. That range is the bandwidth.

Time domain vs frequency domain

Your oscilloscope shows the time domain: voltage vs time. A spectrum analyser shows the frequency domain: power vs frequency. The same signal, viewed differently. When you look at a Wi-Fi transmission on a spectrum analyser, you see a hump — the width of the hump is the channel bandwidth (20/40/80/160 MHz). The height is power. The shape tells you about modulation quality.

// BENCH INSIGHT

Almost every RF measurement is in the frequency domain. RF problems — interference, harmonics, phase noise, spurs — all show up as unexpected energy at unexpected frequencies. A PA generating a second harmonic at 2×f appears as a clear spike on the spectrum analyser. You'd never see it on an oscilloscope.

The concept of a tone

A tone is a single-frequency sinusoidal signal — a pure spike on the spectrum analyser. Most characterisation work starts with a single tone stimulus because it lets you isolate specific behaviour (gain, linearity, noise) before adding the complexity of full OFDM modulation.

// LESSON 1 TAKEAWAYS

  • An RF signal oscillates at a frequency — 2.4 GHz = 2.4 billion cycles per second.
  • Spectrum analyser shows power vs frequency — your primary diagnostic tool.
  • Bandwidth = how much frequency space a signal occupies, set by its modulation.
  • A tone = single-frequency test signal — the starting point for most characterisation.
  • Problems like harmonics and spurs are only visible in the frequency domain.

Why RF engineers think in logarithms

The range of power levels in RF work is enormous. A Wi-Fi transmitter outputs around +20 dBm. A receiver detects signals as weak as −90 dBm. That's a ratio of 10,000,000,000 to 1. Logarithms compress this into a manageable scale. That is the entire motivation.

dBm — absolute power

dBm = decibels relative to 1 milliwatt. 0 dBm = 1 mW. This is your anchor. +20 dBm = 100 mW (typical Wi-Fi Tx power). −90 dBm = 1 picowatt (typical Rx sensitivity). Through a signal chain, just add gains and subtract losses in dB.

Chain CalculationP_out (dBm) = P_in (dBm) + G₁ − L_cable + G₂ − L_filter ...
// PROTECT YOUR INSTRUMENTS

Most spectrum analysers have a max input of +30 dBm. Always calculate the maximum possible power at your instrument before connecting. Use an external attenuator when unsure. A destroyed input mixer is expensive and embarrassing.

dBc = decibels relative to the carrier. Used for spurs, phase noise, harmonics. If carrier is +20 dBm and a spur is −30 dBm, the spur is −50 dBc.

// LESSON 2 TAKEAWAYS

  • dB is a ratio. dBm is absolute power relative to 1 mW.
  • 0 dBm = 1 mW. +10 dB = 10×. +3 dB = 2×. Memorise these.
  • Through a chain: add gains, subtract losses — all in dB.
  • dBc = relative to carrier. Used for spurs, phase noise, harmonics.
  • Never connect high-power signals to instruments without checking max input rating.

Noise — the invisible floor

Every RF measurement has a fundamental limit: thermal noise, generated by the random motion of electrons in any resistive material at any temperature above absolute zero. You cannot eliminate it — only understand and work around it.

Thermal Noise Floor at 290KN₀ = kT = −174 dBm/Hz

This is one of the most important numbers in RF engineering. No signal can be detected below it regardless of how good your receiver is. Every 10× increase in bandwidth raises the noise floor by 10 dB:

Noise in Bandwidth BN (dBm) = −174 + 10·log₁₀(B in Hz)
BandwidthNoise FloorRelevance
1 MHz−114 dBmCommon SA reference
20 MHz−101 dBmWi-Fi 802.11g/n channel
80 MHz−95 dBmWi-Fi 802.11ac/ax channel
160 MHz−92 dBmWide channel 802.11ac/ax

Noise Figure and receiver sensitivity

Every real component adds noise beyond the thermal floor. Noise Figure (NF) quantifies this. An ideal device has NF = 0 dB. A good LNA: ~1.5 dB. A passive 10 dB attenuator: NF = 10 dB.

Receiver SensitivitySensitivity (dBm) = −174 + 10·log₁₀(B) + NF + SNR_min
// ON THE BENCH

The RBW (Resolution Bandwidth) setting on your spectrum analyser controls the noise bandwidth. Narrow RBW lowers the displayed noise floor but slows the sweep. You're not improving the physics — just narrowing the measurement window. Always match RBW to what you're measuring.

// LESSON 3 TAKEAWAYS

  • Thermal noise = −174 dBm/Hz at 290K. Fundamental, unavoidable.
  • Every 10× bandwidth increase raises noise floor 10 dB.
  • Noise Figure = how much extra noise a component adds beyond thermal floor.
  • Receiver sensitivity = thermal floor + NF + minimum SNR required.
  • RBW narrows the measurement window — it doesn't improve the underlying noise.

Impedance — the invisible handshake

Impedance mismatch causes power to reflect back toward the source. Reflected power means less delivered power, distorted waveforms, and potentially damaged transmitters. The entire RF ecosystem is standardised around 50 Ω to prevent this. Every instrument, connector, cable, and PCB trace you'll use is designed for 50 Ω.

Reflection CoefficientΓ = (Z_L − Z₀) / (Z_L + Z₀)
Return LossRL (dB) = −20 · log₁₀|Γ|

S11 on the VNA shows return loss. S11 better than −10 dB means less than 10% of power reflects. Better than −20 dB (1% reflected) is excellent. For well-designed Wi-Fi chipset RF ports: target better than −10 dB across the operating band.

// 50Ω DISCIPLINE ON THE BENCH

Always terminate unused ports with 50 Ω loads. An open port is a perfect reflector and will cause standing waves corrupting every measurement. Use a torque wrench on SMA connectors (0.9 Nm). Inspect connector faces regularly — a damaged pin centre conductor introduces impedance discontinuity and measurement errors at GHz frequencies.

// VNA + ACTIVE DEVICE GOTCHA

Before measuring S11 on an active device, verify the DUT is not transmitting. Transmit power entering your VNA receiver will damage it immediately. Always check DUT state before connecting a VNA.

// LESSON 4 TAKEAWAYS

  • 50 Ω is the universal RF standard — all test equipment assumes it.
  • Impedance mismatch causes reflections — reflected power corrupts measurements.
  • S11 = return loss — how well a port matches 50 Ω. Target better than −10 dB.
  • Always terminate unused ports. Never leave RF ports open.
  • Torque wrench on SMA connectors. Inspect faces regularly.

Module 1 Quiz

Work through each question before revealing the answer. The reasoning matters more than the number.

Q1. Signal generator: +10 dBm → 2 dB cable loss → 20 dB amplifier → 3 dB cable loss → spectrum analyser. What power reaches the SA, and is it safe?
+10 − 2 + 20 − 3 = +25 dBm. Most SAs accept up to +30 dBm. +25 dBm is only 5 dB of margin. If amplifier gain is slightly higher than expected you could destroy the input mixer. On the bench, always add a 10–20 dB pad before the SA when working with amplified signals.
Q2. Wi-Fi receiver: NF = 6 dB, BW = 20 MHz, SNR_min = 10 dB. What is the theoretical sensitivity?
−174 + 10·log(20M) + 6 + 10 = −174 + 73 + 16 = −85 dBm. Each term: −174 = thermal floor per Hz. +73 = noise in 20 MHz BW. +6 = added noise from chipset (NF). +10 = minimum SNR for demodulation. Real 802.11g BPSK specs are typically −82 to −86 dBm — exactly matching this formula.
Q3. You see a spike on the SA at exactly 2× your DUT's transmit frequency. What is this most likely?
Second harmonic. A nonlinear PA generates energy at 2f, 3f, 4f... Phase noise is a skirt around the carrier, not a discrete spike. Image frequencies appear at a different relationship to the carrier. This is why harmonics testing is a regulatory requirement — your transmitter must not pollute other bands.
Q4. You narrow RBW from 100 kHz to 1 kHz on the SA. The displayed noise floor drops 20 dB. Does this mean your measurement is more accurate?
It depends on what you're measuring. For a narrow tone or spur, narrower RBW gives better sensitivity — the signal is fully contained in one RBW bin. For a broadband OFDM signal, narrow RBW shows only a slice of the signal's power — the signal appears weaker too. You're changing the window, not the underlying physics. Always match RBW to the signal being measured.
